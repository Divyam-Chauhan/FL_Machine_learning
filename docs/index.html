Prioritizing content refresh reviews with search and engagement signals

Can a learned ranking help editors find declining content sooner than a transparent rule? A client-held-out comparison on an anonymized starter dataset.

Abstract

This project evaluates whether a Random Forest can rank pages with a historical search-traffic decline label ahead of a hand-built heuristic. On one held-out group of seven clients, model Precision@50 was 0.580, compared with 0.340 for the heuristic; the holdout decline prevalence was 0.511. The result is directional and modest overall (ROC-AUC 0.609). It supports further human-reviewed testing, not an automatic refresh decision or a claim that refreshing causes recovery.

Dataset: 30,000 anonymized content records · 32 pseudonymized client groups · evaluation seed: 42

Question and decision

Research question: Can machine-learned ranking over observable search and engagement signals outperform fixed heuristic rules when prioritizing potentially declining content for editorial review?

Decision supported: Which pages should an editor inspect first when review time is limited? A reviewer can check content freshness, search snippet/CTR, engagement, and editorial context before deciding what—if anything—to change.

The cost of a false positive is wasted editorial attention. A false negative can leave a potentially useful page's decline unreviewed. Human review remains part of the process.

Data and safeguards

The bundled anonymized snapshot contains 30,000 content records across 32 pseudonymized client groups. The analysis uses records with positive 90-day impressions and content age of at least 90 days. The label is whether trend_direction equals down; this is a historical label, not a future forecast.

Label-derived trend_direction and trend_pct are excluded from model inputs. Client IDs are used only to keep groups separate during validation; content IDs are not features. The page publishes aggregate metrics and charts only—no row-level queue, pseudonymous IDs, URLs, titles, client names, or raw queries.

Method

The heuristic combines normalized log impressions, content freshness, and a rank-opportunity score. Its position score is 1.0 for average positions 4–20, 0.5 for positions 1–3, and 0.1 for other or unavailable positions. The notebook originally checked the broad position band first, making its nested top-three case unreachable; that condition order was corrected before these results were regenerated.

The Random Forest uses 100 trees, maximum depth 10, and random seed 42. Its 18 numeric inputs include search volume, impressions/clicks/sessions, content age/freshness, CTR, average position, and engagement measures. Five categorical inputs—competition level, content type, intent, age tier, and position tier—are one-hot encoded, yielding 52 model columns.

A single GroupShuffleSplit with 20% test size and seed 42 held out 7 client groups. Training used 23,837 rows from 25 clients; testing used 6,163 rows from unseen clients. This is one group holdout, not cross-validation or an out-of-fold estimate.

Results on the same holdout

The test-set decline prevalence was 0.511. Precision@K is the share of declining-label records among the top K ranked records.

The model gains 0.240 absolute Precision@50 over the heuristic (about 1.7×). Its Precision@50 is 0.069 above the holdout prevalence, while Precision@20 is below prevalence. ROC-AUC remains modest, so the observed top-50 improvement should not be read as strong general separation.

What the model used

The largest fitted-tree impurity importances were days with impressions (0.1582), log 90-day impressions (0.1160), average position (0.1109), and content age (0.0916). These are relative model split importances—not causal effects—and correlated signals can share or distort importance.

Practical use and limits

Use this as a candidate queue for editors, not as an automated publishing system. Verify each page and its context before acting; record changes and monitor outcomes. In the exploratory full-portfolio queue, aggregate action suggestions were: refresh 10,877; CTR review 9,428; monitor 6,428; engagement review 3,204; expand and refresh 63. These are action-rule counts, not measured improvements.

  • The label describes historical decline; it does not predict a future time period.
  • Only one client-group split was evaluated. Results may vary across clients and splits.
  • ROC-AUC is modest, and missing values are filled with zero in this prototype.
  • SERP features, seasonality, and external changes may explain traffic shifts but are not modeled here.
  • The queue model was fitted on training clients and then scored over the full portfolio; rows from training clients are not out-of-sample predictions.
  • No causal effect of content refresh is estimated. Prospective controlled testing is needed to measure recovery.
Claim boundary: This is an observational, directional decision-support analysis. It does not predict Google's algorithm or prove that a content refresh will recover traffic.
